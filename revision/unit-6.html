Diseases of Rats and Mice

1 · Why this small unit is worth marks

Unit 6 is short but is examined almost entirely through "which disease has this lesion" objective questions. Learn each disease as a triplet: AGENT → SIGNATURE LESION → HOW IT IS DEMONSTRATED. Most answers turn on one named organ, one named body, or one named stain.
The stains that appear again and again in this unit Warthin–Starry silver → Clostridium piliforme (Tyzzer's) and Treponema (rabbit syphilis). Ziehl–Neelsen → mycobacteria. Giemsa → blood parasites and Clostridium piliforme. PAS → fungi and glycogen.

2 · TYZZER'S DISEASE ★★★

  • Agent: Clostridium piliforme (formerly Bacillus piliformis) — a Gram-negative, spore-forming, OBLIGATE INTRACELLULAR rod. It cannot be grown on ordinary cell-free media — this fact alone is a favourite question
  • Hosts: mice, rats, gerbils (the most susceptible), hamsters, rabbits, and also foals, dogs, cats and non-human primates. Precipitated by stress, overcrowding, poor sanitation and corticosteroids; spread by spores in soiled bedding, which survive for years
THE CLASSIC TRIAD — always give all three organs ① LIVER — multiple pinpoint to 2 mm pale yellow-white foci of necrosis scattered through the parenchyma (the commonest and most striking lesion)
② INTESTINE — necrotising ileitis, typhlitis and colitis; watery diarrhoea, soiled perineum, sudden death
③ HEART — pale streaks of myocardial necrosis, especially in the septum and left ventricle
Micro — the diagnostic picture ★ At the margin of the necrotic focus, inside surviving hepatocytes, lie bundles of long, slender, filamentous bacilli arranged in a criss-cross or "haystack/faggot" pattern. They are poorly seen with H&E and Gram, and are demonstrated with WARTHIN–STARRY SILVER stain (the method of choice), Giemsa or PAS.

3 · Pseudotuberculosis & Salmonellosis of rodents

DiseaseAgent & lesion
PSEUDOTUBERCULOSISTwo organisms are described under this heading — know both:
① Yersinia pseudotuberculosis — acquired from contaminated feed, water and wild rodents/birds. Produces caseous, yellow-white "tubercle-like" NODULES in the MESENTERIC LYMPH NODES, intestinal wall, LIVER and SPLEEN, with enteritis and wasting. The nodules resemble tuberculosis but the organisms are NOT acid-fast — hence "pseudo". Zoonotic
② Corynebacterium kutscheri — the classic LATENT infection of rats and mice that becomes clinical only under STRESS, immunosuppression, irradiation or corticosteroids. Produces multiple caseous abscesses in the LUNGS, liver, kidneys, lymph nodes and joints. It is the standard example of an opportunistic latent laboratory-animal infection
SALMONELLOSISSalmonella Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis, from contaminated feed, bedding or wild rodents. Acute: septicaemia and sudden death. Subacute: catarrhal to necrotic enteritis with a distended, congested ileum and caecum, watery diarrhoea, marked SPLENOMEGALY, and multiple grey-white necrotic foci in the LIVER with mesenteric lymphadenitis; abortion and mummification in pregnant animals. An important ZOONOSIS for animal-house staff and a serious contaminant of experimental results

4 · INFECTIOUS ECTROMELIA (mousepox) ★★

  • Ectromelia virus, an ORTHOPOXVIRUS — the mouse equivalent of smallpox. Highly contagious in a colony; spreads through skin abrasions, and also by contaminated tumour lines and biological materials, which is why it is a dreaded laboratory contaminant
  • The name means "missing limb" — from the amputation of extremities in the chronic form
Two very different forms — the examiner picks one ACUTE (visceral) form — the mouse dies in a few days WITHOUT any skin lesion. Post-mortem shows multiple pale foci of NECROSIS in the LIVER and SPLEEN (splenic necrosis is very characteristic), and necrosis of Peyer's patches, lymph nodes and thymus. Mortality can approach 100% in susceptible strains.
CHRONIC (cutaneous) form — in more resistant strains. A generalised rash of papules that ulcerate and scab, with oedema, necrosis and finally SLOUGHING (self-amputation) of the FEET, TAIL and SNOUT — this is "ectromelia"; survivors are deformed and remain carriers.
Micro: epidermal ballooning degeneration and hyperplasia with eosinophilic INTRACYTOPLASMIC inclusion bodies (MARCHAL bodies) in the epithelial cells, and hepatocellular necrosis with intracytoplasmic inclusions.

5 · Infantile diarrhoea · Mouse hepatitis virus · LCM ★★

DiseaseAgent & key point
INFANTILE DIARRHOEA
(epizootic diarrhoea of infant mice, EDIM)		ROTAVIRUS. Affects suckling mice under about 2 weeks only; adults are infected but show nothing. The virus destroys the mature absorptive enterocytes at the VILLUS TIP, sparing the crypts, so the result is VILLOUS ATROPHY with a malabsorptive, osmotic diarrhoea. Clinically: yellow watery faeces, a soiled and pasted perineum ("sticky/scaly tail"), a stomach still distended with curdled milk, and runting. Morbidity is very high but mortality is low — the main loss is stunting and disrupted breeding
MOUSE HEPATITIS VIRUS (MHV)CORONAVIRUS — probably the most prevalent and most troublesome viral contaminant of mouse colonies worldwide. Two groups of strains:
Enterotropic strains — diarrhoea and death in sucklings; SYNCYTIAL (multinucleated) giant cells in the intestinal epithelium.
Polytropic (respiratory) strains — spread from the nose to liver, brain and lymphoid tissue: multifocal hepatic necrosis with characteristic SYNCYTIAL GIANT CELLS, splenic and lymphoid necrosis, vasculitis, and in some strains demyelinating encephalomyelitis.
In immunodeficient (nude/SCID) mice it produces a fatal chronic wasting disease. Its greatest importance is that it alters immune responses and enzyme levels and so invalidates experiments
LYMPHOCYTIC CHORIOMENINGITIS (LCM) ★ARENAVIRUS; the mouse is the natural reservoir and sheds virus in urine, saliva and nasal secretion for life. The classic model of how the age at infection decides the disease:
① Infected in utero or as a neonate → the mouse becomes IMMUNOLOGICALLY TOLERANT and PERSISTENTLY INFECTED with no acute illness; over months the circulating antigen–antibody complexes deposit in the kidney and cause a fatal IMMUNE-COMPLEX (Type III) GLOMERULONEPHRITIS and arteritis — "late disease"
② Infected as an adult → an immune-mediated (T-cell) ACUTE disease: lymphocytic infiltration of the MENINGES, CHOROID PLEXUS and EPENDYMA with convulsions and death. The lesion is produced by the animal's own cytotoxic T cells, not by the virus — the standard demonstration that immunopathology can be the disease
ZOONOTIC — causes aseptic meningitis in people, and is a recognised hazard of handling laboratory mice, hamsters and transplanted tumour lines

6 · GUINEA PIG ★★

SCURVY — the one guinea-pig question you must not lose The guinea pig, like primates, fish and fruit bats, lacks the enzyme L-gulonolactone oxidase and therefore CANNOT SYNTHESISE VITAMIN C — it must get ascorbic acid in the diet.
Vitamin C is needed for the hydroxylation of proline and lysine in collagen synthesis, so deficiency gives defective collagen, osteoid, dentine and capillary basement membrane.
Lesions: haemorrhages into the joints, muscles, periosteum and gums; swollen, painful joints and a reluctance to move ("scorbutic pose"); enlarged, beaded costochondral junctions; loose teeth and bleeding gums; failure of wound healing; anaemia; enlarged epiphyses with a widened, disorganised growth plate. Death from haemorrhage or secondary infection.
ConditionKey point
Cervical lymphadenitis ("LUMPS")Streptococcus equi subsp. zooepidemicus entering through abrasions of the oral mucosa from coarse hay; produces large abscesses of the cervical (submandibular) lymph nodes that may rupture and discharge thick pus; septicaemia in some
PneumoniaThe commonest cause of death in guinea-pig colonies. Bordetella bronchiseptica (often carried silently by rabbits and dogs housed nearby) and Streptococcus pneumoniae; cranioventral consolidation, fibrinous pleuritis, otitis media
ANTIBIOTIC-ASSOCIATED ENTEROTOXAEMIA ★Guinea pigs have a Gram-positive-dominated caecal flora. Giving a narrow-spectrum antibiotic active against Gram-positives — penicillin, ampicillin, lincomycin, clindamycin, erythromycin — wipes it out and allows Clostridium difficile to overgrow. Result: fatal haemorrhagic typhlocolitis within a few days. The same principle applies to rabbits and hamsters
Pregnancy toxaemiaTwo forms: a metabolic (ketotic) form in obese sows in late pregnancy — hypoglycaemia, ketonuria and a large pale fatty liver; and an ischaemic form from compression of the uterine vessels by the gravid uterus and fat
Metastatic calcificationIn older males on diets with low magnesium and a high phosphorus : calcium ratio; gritty white deposits in the kidney, aorta, stomach, lung and skeletal muscle
Malocclusion ("slobbers")Overgrowth of the continuously growing premolar and molar cheek teeth, which trap the tongue; drooling wets the chin and dewlap → moist dermatitis, starvation
EctoparasitesTrixacarus caviae — a sarcoptid mange mite causing intense pruritus, self-trauma and even seizures; lice Gliricola porcelli; ringworm (Trichophyton mentagrophytes)

7 · RABBIT — bacterial diseases ★★

DiseaseAgent & lesion
PASTEURELLOSIS ("SNUFFLES")Pasteurella multocida — the single most important bacterial disease of rabbits, carried subclinically in the nasopharynx and precipitated by poor ventilation, ammonia build-up and stress. It presents in several forms:
① Rhinitis "snuffles" — sneezing and a mucopurulent nasal discharge that mats the fur of the medial forepaws where the rabbit wipes its nose (a diagnostic sign)
② Otitis media/interna → "WRY NECK" (TORTICOLLIS) and loss of balance, with creamy pus in the tympanic bulla
③ Pneumonia and pleuritis — cranioventral consolidation with abscesses
④ Subcutaneous abscesses with thick, white, toothpaste-like inspissated pus (rabbit pus is characteristically caseous, not liquid)
⑤ Genital — pyometra, orchitis; and ⑥ septicaemia with sudden death
"BLUE BREASTS"
(acute septic mastitis)		Chiefly Staphylococcus aureus, also streptococci, entering through a damaged teat in a lactating doe. The mammary glands become hot, hard, swollen and intensely painful, then progress to a dark blue-black CYANOTIC and GANGRENOUS colour — hence the name. The doe is febrile and toxaemic; the litter dies of starvation or of septicaemia acquired while nursing. Abscesses and sloughing follow in survivors
TREPONEMATOSIS
(rabbit syphilis, "vent disease")		Treponema paraluiscuniculi — a VENEREALLY transmitted spirochaete, also passed from doe to kit at birth. It is NOT zoonotic and does NOT cause human syphilis, and it is rarely fatal — both points are often tested.
Lesions: begin as erythema, oedema and small vesicles on the vulva, prepuce, scrotum and anus, which become dry, brown, crusted and scaly scabs that bleed when removed. By self-inoculation while grooming, identical lesions appear on the nose, lips and eyelids. Causes infertility and metritis.
Diagnosis: SILVER (Warthin–Starry) stain or dark-field microscopy of a scraping; the organism cannot be cultured
ENTEROTOXAEMIAClostridium spiroforme and its iota toxin. Affects mainly weanling rabbits of 4–8 weeks, precipitated by a low-fibre, high-carbohydrate diet, sudden diet change, stress or oral antibiotics.
Lesions: the CAECUM is distended with dark, watery, foul-smelling fluid and gas; the caecal and colonic mucosa shows petechial and "paint-brush" haemorrhages; serosal haemorrhages. Death is often sudden, in the best-grown rabbits

8 · RABBIT — viral and parasitic diseases ★★★

DiseaseAgent & lesion
RABBIT POXAn ORTHOPOXVIRUS closely related to vaccinia; essentially a disease of laboratory rabbit colonies, spread by aerosol and contact. Fever, then a generalised eruption of macules and papules over the whole skin, with marked OEDEMA of the eyelids, face, lips and the anogenital region, blepharoconjunctivitis with a purulent discharge, and enlarged lymph nodes. Internally there is focal necrosis in the lung, liver, spleen and testis. Mortality is high. Intracytoplasmic inclusions
INFECTIOUS MYXOMATOSIS ★★★MYXOMA VIRUS, a LEPORIPOXVIRUS. Transmitted MECHANICALLY by biting arthropods — mosquitoes and the rabbit flea (so outbreaks are seasonal). The natural host is the American cottontail, in which it causes only a benign fibroma; in the EUROPEAN rabbit (Oryctolagus) it is devastating, with mortality approaching 100% — this is the virus deliberately released in Australia to control rabbits.
Gross: severe gelatinous OEDEMA of the eyelids, base of the ears, nose, muzzle and the ANOGENITAL region, producing the classic swollen, drooping "LION-HEAD" appearance; a thick purulent blepharoconjunctivitis glues the eyes shut; and multiple raised, rubbery, gelatinous cutaneous tumour-like masses called "MYXOMAS", especially on the ears and face.
Micro: the dermis is expanded by a loose, mucinous (myxomatous) matrix containing large stellate "MYXOMA CELLS", with epidermal hyperplasia, ballooning degeneration and eosinophilic INTRACYTOPLASMIC inclusion bodies
PAPILLOMATOSISTwo distinct viruses:
① Shope (cottontail) papillomavirus — horny, keratinised, wart-like growths on the skin of the neck, shoulders and abdomen; historically important because these papillomas can progress to SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA, making this the first experimental model of viral carcinogenesis in a mammal.
② Rabbit oral papillomavirus — small, grey-white, sessile nodules on the UNDERSIDE OF THE TONGUE and floor of the mouth of young rabbits; benign and self-limiting
COCCIDIOSIS ★The commonest parasitic disease of rabbits. Two forms — always answer both:
① HEPATIC coccidiosis — Eimeria stiedae, which is unique in parasitising the EPITHELIUM OF THE BILE DUCTS. The liver is enlarged and studded with sharply outlined white to yellow nodules and cords that follow the course of the bile ducts; the ducts are thick-walled and full of creamy material packed with oocysts. Micro: marked bile-duct (biliary) epithelial HYPERPLASIA with papillary projections, and all stages of the parasite inside the epithelial cells. Icterus, ascites, and death in young rabbits.
② INTESTINAL coccidiosis — E. magna, E. irresidua, E. perforans, E. flavescens — catarrhal to haemorrhagic enteritis, diarrhoea, dehydration and poor growth
MITE INFESTATIONPsoroptes cuniculi — "EAR CANKER", by far the commonest. Thick, dry, greyish-brown, crumbly, flaky crusts filling the ear canal and pinna; intense irritation with head shaking and ear scratching; may extend to otitis media/interna and torticollis, or become secondarily infected.
Cheyletiella parasitovorax — "WALKING DANDRUFF", large surface-living mites causing scurf and mild alopecia over the shoulders and back; ZOONOTIC, giving an itchy rash on handlers.
Sarcoptes scabiei and Notoedres — burrowing mange of nose, lips and feet with thick crusts and intense pruritus

9 · VIRAL DISEASES OF WILD ANIMALS ★★

DiseaseAgent & pathology in wildlife
WEST NILE FEVERFLAVIVIRUS, transmitted by Culex mosquitoes. Wild BIRDS are the amplifying/reservoir hosts; corvids (crows, jays, magpies) die in large numbers and a die-off of crows is the classic early warning of an outbreak. Horses and humans are DEAD-END hosts (they do not develop enough viraemia to infect a mosquito).
Lesions: in birds — myocardial necrosis, splenomegaly, encephalitis, haemorrhage. In horses — non-suppurative POLIOENCEPHALOMYELITIS concentrated in the GREY MATTER of the BRAIN STEM and SPINAL CORD, with perivascular cuffing, gliosis, neuronal necrosis and microglial nodules; clinically ataxia, muscle fasciculation and recumbency. Zoonotic
RABIESSame Lyssavirus, but in wildlife the epidemiology differs: the reservoirs in India include the jackal, fox, mongoose and stray dog, and elsewhere bats, raccoons and skunks. Wild carnivores show the furious form — loss of fear of man, daytime activity, aggression, aimless wandering — and herbivores such as deer show the dumb/paralytic form. Same pathology: non-suppurative polioencephalitis with NEGRI BODIES; diagnosis by FAT on brain. Rabies in a wild animal that has bitten people is a public-health emergency
FOOT AND MOUTH DISEASEAffects wild cloven-footed species — gaur, deer (chital, sambar), blackbuck, nilgai, wild boar and Asian elephants. Same lesions: vesicles and erosions on the tongue, dental pad, interdigital cleft and coronary band, lameness, and "TIGER HEART" myocarditis in the young, which causes the deaths. Outbreaks in national parks usually spill over from infected livestock grazing at the forest fringe — an important interface problem in India
POXCamelpox in camels; buffalopox (zoonotic, teat and udder lesions); MONKEYPOX in non-human primates and rodents, a serious zoonosis; elephant pox; and capripox in wild ruminants. All show the same sequence macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → scab with ballooning degeneration and INTRACYTOPLASMIC inclusion bodies
KYASANUR FOREST DISEASE ★★
("monkey fever")		A FLAVIVIRUS first recognised in 1957 in the Kyasanur forest of Shimoga district, KARNATAKA, INDIA — so it is a favourite Indian question. Transmitted by the hard tick Haemaphysalis spinigera; small forest rodents and shrews maintain it, and monkeys — the black-faced langur and the red-faced bonnet macaque — die in large numbers. A monkey die-off in the forest precedes human cases, so it is used as a sentinel.
Lesions: a haemorrhagic fever — widespread haemorrhages in the gut, lungs and serosae, focal necrosis of the liver and kidney, interstitial pneumonia, and erythrophagocytosis in the spleen, liver and lymph nodes. In people it causes a severe haemorrhagic fever; forest workers and veterinarians are at risk
INFECTIOUS HEPATITIS VIRUSCanine adenovirus-1 in wild carnivores — foxes, wolves, jackals, coyotes and bears. In addition to the usual centrilobular hepatic necrosis with Cowdry type A intranuclear inclusions and gall-bladder wall oedema, it characteristically produces "FOX ENCEPHALITIS" — vasculitis of CNS vessels with haemorrhage and non-suppurative encephalitis, giving convulsions and paralysis. Related canine adenovirus-2 causes kennel cough

10 · BACTERIAL DISEASES OF WILD ANIMALS ★★

DiseasePathology in wildlife
ANTHRAXThe classic cause of mass die-offs of wild herbivores — grazing animals ingest spores concentrated at drying waterholes and on disturbed soil, especially at the end of a dry season. Vultures, hyaenas and flies then spread the spores widely.
Same rules as in livestock and they are always examined: a wild herbivore found dead with dark tarry unclotted blood from the natural orifices, absent or incomplete rigor mortis and a huge soft "blackberry-jam" spleen must NOT be opened in the field. Take a peripheral blood smear and stain by polychrome methylene blue for McFADYEAN'S reaction. Dispose by incineration or deep burial with quicklime; the carcass site remains infective for decades. Carnivores are relatively resistant but may get the pharyngeal/intestinal form
TUBERCULOSISMycobacterium bovis in deer, wild boar, bison and antelope — often maintained in a wildlife reservoir that reinfects cattle. Captive ELEPHANTS are important because they usually get M. tuberculosis FROM PEOPLE — a REVERSE zoonosis, presenting as chronic weight loss with a trunk-wash-positive culture. Lesions are the usual caseous granulomas with epithelioid and Langhans giant cells in lung and lymph nodes, but calcification is often minimal in deer, in which the lesions may be soft and abscess-like. Confirmed by Ziehl–Neelsen and culture
COLIBACILLOSISE. coli in hand-reared and captive-born neonates, almost always secondary to FAILURE OF PASSIVE TRANSFER when the young is separated from the dam or fed an unsuitable milk replacer. Produces watery diarrhoea and dehydration, and in the septicaemic form omphalitis, polyarthritis, meningitis and fibrinous polyserositis. A leading cause of death in zoo nurseries
CLOSTRIDIAL INFECTIONSEnterotoxaemia (Cl. perfringens) in captive and hand-reared ungulates after sudden access to rich concentrate — pulpy kidney, haemorrhagic enteritis. Blackleg (Cl. chauvoei) in wild bovids — dark crepitant muscle. Tetanus after wounds, snare injuries and darting. BOTULISM (Cl. botulinum type C) is the great one in wildlife — it causes massive die-offs of WATERFOWL at shallow, warm, drying wetlands, where the birds eat maggots from rotting carcasses; they show flaccid paralysis of the neck ("LIMBERNECK"), wings and legs and drown, and there are no gross lesions

11 · PARASITIC DISEASES OF WILD ANIMALS

DiseaseKey point in wildlife
TRYPANOSOMOSISTrypanosoma evansi (surra) is the important one in India — it affects camels most severely, and also deer, tigers, leopards and elephants in captivity, where infected buffalo or camels nearby act as the source and Tabanus flies transmit it MECHANICALLY. Big cats often acquire it by eating infected meat. Signs: intermittent fever, progressive anaemia and cachexia, ventral oedema, dullness, and terminal nervous signs. Post-mortem: splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, serous atrophy of fat, pale carcass, hydropericardium and non-suppurative meningoencephalitis. Diagnosis: wet blood film for motile organisms, Giemsa-stained smear, mouse inoculation, PCR
BABESIOSISBabesia species in wild bovids, deer and wild canids, transmitted by ixodid ticks. Free-living wildlife is usually in endemic stability and shows no disease, acting as a reservoir. Clinical disease appears in stressed, translocated, captured or immunosuppressed animals — fever, intravascular haemolysis with HAEMOGLOBINURIA, jaundice, anaemia and splenomegaly. Diagnosis: pear-shaped paired piroplasms inside the red cells on a Giemsa-stained smear
THEILERIOSISTheileria species in wild bovids and deer, transmitted by Hyalomma and Rhipicephalus ticks. Marked lymph node enlargement, fever, anaemia and jaundice, "punched-out" abomasal ulcers, and pulmonary oedema. Diagnosis by KOCH'S BLUE BODIES (schizonts) in lymphocytes of a lymph-node aspirate. As with babesiosis, wildlife often carries the parasite silently and infects introduced exotic or crossbred stock

12 · NUTRITIONAL DEFICIENCY DISEASES OF WILD ANIMALS ★★

Almost all of these are diseases of CAPTIVITY — a free-ranging animal selects a balanced diet, a caged or hand-reared one eats what it is given.

DeficiencyDisease produced
Calcium / phosphorus / vitamin D
(NUTRITIONAL SECONDARY HYPERPARATHYROIDISM) ★		The commonest and most important nutritional disease of captive wildlife. Caused by an ALL-MEAT diet in hand-reared carnivores (muscle meat is very low in calcium and high in phosphorus, giving a badly inverted Ca : P ratio), and by lack of sunlight in indoor enclosures. Low ionised calcium → sustained parathyroid hormone release → bone is resorbed and replaced by fibrous tissue = FIBROUS OSTEODYSTROPHY.
Lesions: soft, thin, poorly mineralised bones with pathological folding fractures, bowed limbs, a rubbery mandible, collapsed vertebrae with paralysis, reluctance to move, and tetany. Classic in hand-reared tiger and leopard cubs, primates and captive reptiles. Called rickets if the growth plates are still open, osteomalacia in adults
Vitamin ACommon in captive carnivores, primates and reptiles on poor diets: night blindness and xerophthalmia, squamous metaplasia and keratinisation of glandular epithelium, respiratory infection, poor growth, and in reptiles the classic swollen closed eyelids from squamous metaplasia of the harderian gland. Conversely, hypervitaminosis A from an all-liver diet causes deforming cervical spondylosis in captive cats
Vitamin E / seleniumWHITE MUSCLE DISEASE / nutritional muscular dystrophy in captive ungulates, especially deer, antelope and zoo-reared calves on stored feed: bilaterally symmetrical chalky white streaks in skeletal muscle and heart, Zenker's degeneration, ↑ CK and AST. Also steatitis ("yellow fat disease") in fish-eating animals and captive felids
THIAMINE (B₁) ★Occurs in animals fed large amounts of RAW FISH, which contains THIAMINASE — captive fish-eating birds, otters, seals, dolphins, crocodiles and fur animals. This is "CHASTEK PARALYSIS": anorexia, ataxia, head tremor, opisthotonus/star-gazing and convulsions, and in ruminants POLIOENCEPHALOMALACIA with laminar cortical necrosis that autofluoresces under UV light. Prevented by cooking the fish or supplementing thiamine
Vitamin CScurvy in captive primates, guinea pigs, fruit bats and some fish, which cannot synthesise ascorbic acid — haemorrhages, painful swollen joints, gum disease and failure of wound healing
Iodine · copper · cobalt · ironGoitre in captive ungulates and reptiles on goitrogenic diets; copper deficiency → ataxia, achromotrichia and anaemia in wild ruminants on deficient range; iron deficiency anaemia in hand-reared young
Protein–energy malnutritionFree-ranging wildlife in drought or overstocked habitat: emaciation, SEROUS ATROPHY OF FAT in the coronary groove, perirenal region and bone marrow (the marrow becomes gelatinous and watery — the standard field indicator of starvation), hypoproteinaemic oedema and heavy parasite burdens
Not nutritional, but the wildlife lesion you must know ★ CAPTURE MYOPATHY (exertional rhabdomyolysis) — follows the chase, capture, restraint or transport of a wild animal, especially deer, antelope, blackbuck and zebra. Extreme exertion with catecholamine release causes lactic acidosis and Zenker's (coagulative) necrosis of skeletal and cardiac muscle: pale, dry, chalky-white streaked muscle, myoglobinuria with dark brown urine and renal tubular necrosis, and death hours to days later. Prevention — minimise chase time, use proper chemical immobilisation, avoid the heat of the day.

13 · Necropsy of laboratory and wild animals — the practical rules

  • Legal position: a wild animal necropsy needs the authority of the Forest Department under the Wild Life (Protection) Act; it is usually done in the presence of forest officials, and in a suspected poaching or poisoning case it is treated exactly like a veterolegal post-mortem — photographs, numbered samples, seals and a documented chain of custody
  • Always exclude ANTHRAX first in any herbivore found dead — blood smear before the knife
  • Look for human interference: snare and wire marks, bullet or pellet wounds (radiograph if possible), missing claws, teeth, horn, skin or gall bladder, and evidence of deliberate poisoning of a carcass — carnivore deaths in India are commonly due to pesticide-laced baits, so collect stomach content, liver and kidney frozen, with NO preservative, for toxicology
  • Decomposition is usually advanced in field cases; interpret cautiously and separate post-mortem change from lesion
  • Laboratory animals: the value of the necropsy is often to detect subclinical colony infections that invalidate experiments (MHV, LCM, Corynebacterium kutscheri). Perform under sterile conditions; sample for serology and PCR as well as histopathology, and remember that LCM, salmonellosis, and Cheyletiella are ZOONOSES
